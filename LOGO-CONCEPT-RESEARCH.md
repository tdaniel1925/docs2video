# Logo concept research — the framework Logo Studio must implement

Researched September 2026. Sources at the end. Where evidence was thin it says so.

## The core finding: literal marks are legally weak, not just dull

The strongest argument against "AI company gets a robot" is not taste, it is **ownability**.

Trademark law grades marks on a **spectrum of distinctiveness**: generic → descriptive → suggestive → arbitrary → fanciful. Generic marks "cannot be afforded any legal protection." Descriptive marks are protectable only after acquiring secondary meaning through years of spend. Suggestive, arbitrary and fanciful marks are registrable immediately. This applies to images, not only words — "a pine tree shape is descriptive when used on pine-scented products."

**A robot for an AI company is the visual equivalent of naming a salt company "Salt."** Every competitor can use it. A literal mark fails the one job a mark has: being ownable. That sentence belongs in the prompt.

## How the famous marks actually work

| Mark | What it depicts | Relationship to the business |
|---|---|---|
| Nike | Wing of the goddess Nike | Metaphor for victory — not a shoe |
| Chase | Abstract octagon | "Merely intended to be distinctive and geometric" |
| Apple | Bitten apple | Arbitrary; the bite is a scale cue, "so it would not be confused with a cherry" |
| FedEx | Arrow in negative space | An attribute (speed, precision), not a truck |
| NBC | Peacock | Metaphor for colour broadcasting — the attribute, not the business |
| Amazon | a-to-z arrow / smile | Wordplay on the NAME, not the trade |
| Target | Bullseye | Literal to the NAME, not the business |
| Shell | Pecten scallop | Literal — to an 1833 seashell-trading business, not oil |

Two nuances that matter for the prompt:
- Target and Amazon show punning on the **name** is legitimate — but only when the name is not the trade. "BotMakers" makes robots the trade, so the pun collapses into description.
- Shell is the case people cite for literalism and it proves the reverse: a fossil of a former meaning that now works as pure abstract shape.

**When literal is right:** low-consideration retail and hospitality where instant category legibility beats ownability, and heritage marks where the object genuinely is the story. So allow at most one literal concept — never nine.

## What the professional process actually is

The verifiable practitioner process (Ian Paget / Logo Geek) does its divergent work **in language and meaning, before any mark exists**:

- **Word mapping** — the business at the centre, branching outward. His British Overland case ran "British" → … → **lion**. The final mark contains no word from the brief.
- **Picture mapping** — sketching icons rather than listing words.
- **Storytelling** — the Soul Somatic mark uses two swirls for experiencing trauma twice, with radiating lines for release.
- **Sketch everything, including the rubbish ideas** — explicitly refusing to stop at the first solution.

Our pipeline had no such step. Handing the model a name and a description guarantees the first-solution failure, nine times over.

## Testable criteria for a good mark

- **Ownability** — where it sits on generic→fanciful. The single best filter.
- **Distinctiveness within the category** — measured against real competitor marks, not in the abstract.
- **Small-size and one-colour survival** — registration protects the design independent of colour, so the silhouette must carry it alone.
- **Recall vs recognition** — "it is 8 to 10 times more difficult to recall a brand than recognize one."
- **Timelessness** — absence of trend markers.

## What every competing AI tool does — and the opening

**None of them generate concepts.** Tailor Brands claims "we don't use pre-made logo templates" while running a questionnaire → icon-pick → font-pick → colour-match pipeline: permutation over a fixed icon set. LogoAI advertises its variation axis explicitly as *style* — "bold athletic, minimal line-art, flat illustrated, vintage badge, luxury serif." Recraft optimises for the opposite of diversity: "style it once, every image matches."

Every competitor varies **style over one idea** — exactly the failure identified in our own output. A tool that varies **idea** is genuinely differentiated at $149–299.

## The framework to implement

### Mechanism A — interrogate the business, not the name

Before ideating, answer:
1. What does the customer actually *get*? (the outcome, not the service)
2. What changes for them — before versus after?
3. What attribute should this brand own? (speed, precision, craft, scale)
4. What is the founding story or origin?
5. What do competitors' marks look like? (to avoid)
6. What does the name literally mean — and what is it a *metaphor* for?

### Mechanism B — a hard ban

No concept may depict the literal noun in the business name or its immediate synonyms. Derive the blocklist from the brief. For BotMakers: no robots, androids, circuit boards, chat bubbles, brains, gears.

### Mechanism C — nine mandated slots, one idea each

Force each concept from a **different generative technique**, so variety is structural rather than hoped for:

1. **Attribute metaphor** (the NBC peacock move)
2. **Process / mechanism abstraction** — what the work *does*, geometrically
3. **Letterform manipulation** of an initial
4. **Negative-space double meaning** (FedEx)
5. **Outcome / transformation** — before→after as a shape
6. **Adjacent-category borrowing** — cartography, textiles, music notation
7. **Heritage / craft reference**
8. **Pure geometric abstraction**, ownable by form alone (Chase)
9. **Monogram / seal system**

Each concept states: the idea in one sentence, why it fits, the visual description, and its technique slot. Then, and only then, render.

## Worked example — the real failing brief

*BotMakers — AI Powered Software and Web Solutions.* Attribute to own: **things built, not things automated.** The name says "bot"; the business is *making*. Ban: robots, androids, circuits, chips, chat bubbles.

1. **Attribute metaphor — the anvil.** A minimal anvil silhouette; craft and force, zero tech cliché.
2. **Process abstraction — assembly.** Four small squares converging into one solid square. Components becoming a system.
3. **Letterform — the double-M gate.** The "M" of Makers cut so two verticals form an open doorway; software as a way through.
4. **Negative space — the B/arrow.** A "B" whose counters leave a forward-pointing arrow in the gap.
5. **Outcome — tangle to line.** A knotted line at left resolving into one clean stroke at right. Complexity made simple.
6. **Adjacent borrowing — the maker's punch mark.** A silversmith's hallmark: a stamped lozenge with an abstract glyph. Workshop provenance, not Silicon Valley.
7. **Heritage — the printer's press bar.** A slab-serif wordmark with one heavy horizontal rule, echoing letterpress. Web *publishing* lineage.
8. **Pure geometry — the interlock.** Two identical hexagonal forms sharing an edge, forming a third shape. The Chase move.
9. **Monogram seal — BM circle.** A tight circular monogram, B and M sharing a stem, working as a stamp of approval.

None is a robot. Each comes from a different technique, so they cannot collapse into one another.

## Evidence gaps, stated plainly

- Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood, "Let the Logo Do the Talking" (reportedly *Journal of Marketing Research* 2019), which reportedly found descriptive logos raise brand evaluations, **could not be fetched through eight routes**. It is the main pro-literal counter-evidence. Treat as unverified; it matters directionally — literal is not *always* wrong, it is wrong when nine of nine are literal and identical.
- Quotes attributed to Paul Rand, Sagi Haviv, David Airey and Michael Bierut could not be sourced and are deliberately not quoted.
- Designer-community critique of AI logo tools was inaccessible (Reddit and G2 blocked).
- Ehrenberg-Bass distinctive-asset theory is the relevant body of work on "stand out vs fit in" but could not be verified live — do not cite as fact.
